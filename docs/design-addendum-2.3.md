## 1. The cruelty of every tradition (Books 1, 4, 5, 6, 8)

Every religious tradition in the game is shown with its cruelty as well as its comfort, even-
handedly, through what its believers do. No tradition is the good one.

| Tradition | Cruelty shown in play |
|---|---|
| Norse belief | Thralls killed to follow a chieftain into death; Heid giving captured crews to Rán; blood-oaths that bind sons to their fathers' feuds; Orm drowning hostages whose families failed to pay |
| The Church at Kilbrecc | Bondmen who work the tithe farms and cannot leave; public floggings for penance; the abbot's curse (a fast and a malediction against a household, the Gaelic custom of fasting against an enemy); hired swords when prayer fails; shrines of the older faith smashed |
| Gaelic custom | Satire that can ruin a person; the blood-price that lets the rich kill the poor; hostages starved when treaties break; geasa that doom people to impossible choices |
| The Keepers and the old powers | Seal-kin women bound by whoever holds their skin; the sick left on a skerry for the sea to judge; the Bog King's drowned offerings in the peat |

Rules: shown, not glorified; always with believers who are kind, doubtful or ashamed too. Hard
limits remain: no children as actors, no sexual violence, no player torture.

## 2. Ordinary people, period ethics, and who gets depth (Books 5, 6, 8, 10)

- **Most people are ordinary**, with simple motives: food, safety, family, money, pride, fear,
  a grudge, a debt. Avoid making minor characters schemers or philosophers.
- **Minor characters** (farmers, traders, guards, travellers) have one or two lines, drawn from
  pools that vary by culture, season, time of day, weather and what the household has done.
- **Major characters**, meaning leaders, antagonists, residents and quest characters, have
  **branching conversation and reaction trees** that change with:
  - time of day and season (a different greeting at dawn, at a winter fire, on a feast day);
  - the household's deeds and reputation;
  - their happiness, friendship and fear towards the steerer;
  - quest and story state;
  - recent events (a death, a raid, a storm).
- **Minimum depth for each major character:** greetings for each season and time of day;
  at least three topic trees; reactions to at least ten deed types; callbacks to remembered
  events; a change in voice when friendship or fear crosses a threshold.

### Period and culture ethics
- **Everyone judges by the ethics of their own culture around AD 900, the household included.**
  Right and wrong are spoken of as honour and shame, luck, oaths, kin and vengeance, hospitality,
  blood-price, sin and penance, and what the gods or saints will do. Never in modern terms.
- **Compassion is real, but it speaks in period terms.** A Mercy-minded Norse rower doesn't say
  a killing was wrong because the man was innocent. She says there was no need of it, that it
  brings bad luck, that his kin will come, that it shamed the crew. A monk calls it a sin.
  A Gael says his honour-price must be paid.
- **The same deed reads differently in each culture.** Witness reactions are weighted by the
  witness's culture, then by their values (Book 5), traits (section 5) and stance (below).

| Deed | Norse (household, Orm, Ulf) | Gaels (Dún Fiach, Ardcarn, Glen Arra) | Church (Kilbrecc) | Keepers (Inis Rón) |
|---|---|---|---|---|

  the scale is the great shame, and killing is bad for trade more than it is wrong. People of
  mixed birth (Bran, Senán, Áine and much of Saltvik) carry both codes, and judge and are judged
  by either.
- **Each major character has a stance towards their own culture's code:** conventional (most
  people), sceptical (doubts the gods, saints or the code's worth, but mostly keeps it), cynical
  (uses the code's words for gain) or transgressive (openly breaks a central rule, and knows it).
  Stance shapes how people justify themselves and what they say aloud. Values say what they care
  about; traits say how they behave. Stances can shift after strong events, as values do.

- **Period views are spoken; the hard limits still hold in the systems.** Characters may treat
  thralls, the slave market, arranged marriages, blood-feud and harsh penance as ordinary, and
  some will urge the steerer to take captives for sale. The game still has no slave-market loop,
  and captives are never currency. Period attitudes to women and men exist in the world (Eithne's
  marriage, Órlaith's), but no line applies them to the steerer or reveals the steerer's gender
  (Book 8 section 03).
- **The steerer's choices are period voices too.** Dialogue options cover the range a person of
  the time might take (conventional, pragmatic, merciful, cruel, transgressive), each phrased
  with a period reason: "Dead men pay no silver." "Let their God judge them."
  "We are not Orm." The player can act against the household's code; the household reacts by
  that code.
- **Existing reactions are re-voiced, not reversed.** The residents Book 5 describes as shaken by
  killing civilians or the surrendered still are; their reasons become period reasons. Review
  Book 10's deltas so that, for example, taking captives in a fight is ordinary to most Norse
  residents (Mercy objects to cruelty towards captives, not to capture), and theft from the
  household's own stores is judged more harshly than open plunder abroad.
- **Lint (Books 2 and 8):** the banned list for all dialogue strings gains modern moral
  vocabulary: rights, war crime, genocide, trauma, civilian, ethics, morality, values, consent,
  abuse, victim, tolerance, equality. Dialogue says "farmers", "folk" or "people who bore no
  weapon" rather than "civilians"; "innocent" is allowed (it is a Church word). UI text such as
  the household screen is exempt.

## 3. Journeys: road legs, wilderness routes and points of interest (Books 1, 2, 4, 7, 9, 10; replaces the walk-between-areas model in 2.2 section 3)

### How a journey works
- **The mainland has two kinds of playable place:**
  - **Sites:** destinations explored in full on foot (villages, forts, the monastery, farms,
    dungeons, the bog village, the mountain).
  - **Wilderness routes:** long playable areas *between* sites. **Whenever a journey passes
    through a route, the player crosses it on foot, in game.**
- **Road legs** join sites and route ends. They happen off screen and cost game time.
- A journey is a chain of these. For example, Ardcarn → two hours on the road (off screen) →
  **the Black Wood path** (played: about 2.5 game hours on its direct path, longer if you
  explore) → one hour on the road (off screen) → Móin Mhór.
- **A day fits one or two nearby sites.** At 50 real seconds per game hour (16.7 real minutes from
  06:00 to 02:00), far sites need a camp on the way, or the ship.
- **The ship is the fast way along the coast.** Coastal sites (Carrick, Kilbrecc, Dún Fiach's
  landing, the salt pans, Saltvik, Saltness) can be reached by sea, on the sea map with its own
  risks, skipping the inland routes. Inland places can only be reached through the routes.

### Road legs (off screen)
- **Modifiers:** night x1.5 and riskier; driving cattle or carrying cargo x1.5; known roads x0.8;
  the kelpie bridle halves water crossings; blocks (landslide, spate, snow, flood) force detours or
  close the way, as in 2.2.
- **Journey events:** a road leg can be interrupted by a short playable scene: travellers,
  bandits, wolves, weather, a wounded stranger, or a discovery ("smoke in the hills" adds a new
  point of interest). Most road legs are quiet.

### Wilderness routes (played)
- **Shape:** long rather than square, with a clear main path, side paths, pockets, one or two
  camp spots and at least one shortcut to find. The direct path takes about 1.5 to 2.5 real
  minutes of walking (2 to 3 game hours); exploring off it can double that. Time runs at the
  normal rate.
- **Contents:** one major point of interest, 4 to 8 minor ones, wildlife always, gathering spots
  (herbs, nuts, berries, timber, peat, fish in streams), hazards, and camp spots.
- **Each crossing is different:** time of day, weather, season, and the living-world deck (2.2
  section 6):
  - wandering people: travellers, pilgrims, pedlars, herders, charcoal burners, patrols, outlaws,
    a lost traveller, a fleeing thrall, a hunting party;
  - wildlife: deer, boar, hares, wolves, ravens, otters, eagles, by season;
  - risk of attack: bandits, outlaws, wolves, a boar, or a patrol of a hostile faction.
- **Encounter rates (PROPOSAL; numbers in Book 10):** wandering people on about half of
  crossings; a hostile encounter on about one crossing in five by day and one in three at night;
  modified by season, faction state, cargo carried and whether the household has cleared the
  route.
- **Ambushes are readable:** a fallen tree across the path, crows rising ahead, a stretch gone too
  quiet, fresh tracks. Keen-eyed companions spot them sooner.
- **Making a route better:** find its shortcuts; drive out the outlaws who use it; waymark it;
  bridge the ford (2.2); and (PROPOSAL) build a **waystation**, a safe camp with a small store, at
  one camp spot per route. Routes held by a hostile faction stay open but dangerous.
- **Cargo and cattle** slow the crossing and draw raiders. Companions cross with the steerer.
- **Each route has its own look** and must meet the art standard set at Stage 6 (wind, moving
  plants, light, water and wildlife):

### Points of interest in every site and route
- **Major points of interest:** one or two per site, one per route: a village, dungeon, ruin,
  shrine or camp with people and quests.
- **Minor points of interest:** 4 to 8 per site or route: a cairn, a wreck, a carved stone, a
  hermit's hut, a hidden cache, a viewpoint, an eagle's nest, a herb patch, a grave, a burned
  farm.
- **Off the beaten track:** the best minor rewards lie off the obvious paths, behind a climb, a
  wade, a hazard or a puzzle, and reward curiosity with food, materials, lore, collectibles or
  equipment.
- **Hazards** (as in 2.2) guard many of the best finds.
- **Camping:** if night falls away from home, camp at a site, a route's camp spot or a waystation
  (as in 2.2).

## 4. Story gates instead of fixed dates (Books 1, 3, 4, 10)

- **The story moves forward only through major events the player chooses to start**: a raid,
  an expedition, a defence, a council. These are **gates**.
- **The calendar keeps running.** Seasons turn, crops grow and winter must be prepared for, but
  no chapter deadline passes without the player: dated story events wait at their gate.
  Pressure builds gently while the player delays (demands grow, rumours spread), but never
  forces a gate.
- **Each gate has a readiness screen** (crew, ship, stores, allies, equipment) and a clear warning
  that it is a point of no return.
- **Gates (PROPOSAL):** the launch (end of Chapter 1); the Carrick raid; the reply to Asgrim; the
  defence of Skarvholm; the household council; the move ashore; the Gathering; the Return to
  Skarvholm; the final battle.
- Chapter windows in Book 4 become **typical** dates rather than limits. The Return's "one retry
  before day 255" becomes "one retry before you set out for the final battle".
- Book 10's ideal progression stays as a guide for a well-paced player, not a deadline.

## 5. People like Dwarf Fortress: traits, likes, relationships, tendencies (Books 2, 5, 8, 10)

Every resident and companion has:
- **Traits (two to four)**, some visible at once and some discovered over time. Starting set:

| Trait | Effect |
|---|---|
| Berserk | In combat, when wounded or when a friend falls, may go berserk: ignores orders, attacks the nearest enemy wildly (including those surrendering, and civilians in reach), deals more damage, defends less. Deeds count against the household |
| Cowardly | Breaks first when cohesion falls; may flee a fight; hates night raids |
| Superstitious | Refuses tasks or voyages after bad omens; panics at supernatural signs; insists on rites |
| Brave | Holds the line longer; volunteers for danger |
| Greedy | Pockets loot; argues over shares |
| Pious (to a given faith) | Prays daily; reacts strongly to sacrilege and to its own faith's cruelty |
| Hot-tempered | Starts disputes; quick to fight |
| Gentle | Calms disputes; hates killing |
| Drinker | Drinks at feasts and in hard times; unreliable the morning after |
| Light-fingered | Steals small things from the stores (Oddi) |
| Keen-eyed | Spots things first at sea and on watch |
| Sea-sick | Worse at sea; better on land |
| Storyteller or singer | Raises happiness at the fire |
| Grudge-holder | Remembers every slight; feuds |
| Diligent or lazy | Works more or less without supervision |

- **Likes and dislikes:** foods (salt fish, honey, ale, kale), activities (fishing, carving,
  fighting), places, weather, people and gifts. Meeting them raises happiness; ignoring them
  does not hurt, but dislikes do.
- **Relationships between residents** form and break through shared events: friends, rivals,
  grudges, mentors, drinking companions, family. No romance system; existing marriages and
  family ties remain.
- **Tendencies:** small daily behaviours that make people readable: one prays at dawn, one
  wanders the shore at night, one hoards stones, one sings at the oars.
- Traits affect work, combat behaviour, reactions to deeds, disputes and conversation.
- The household screen shows each person's known traits, likes, relationships, happiness,
  friendship and fear, with a line on why each is what it is.

## 6. Optional quests for equipment and requests; companions (Books 1, 4, 6)

- **Optional quests** can be mounted at any time for: ship enhancements (a better sail, a carved
  prow, a sea-charm, a famous mast timber), character equipment (the dungeon rewards in 2.2,
  plus smaller finds), and residents' requests (2.2 section 9).
- **Companions:** the steerer can take up to two friendly characters on any expedition on foot.
  They fight, help with puzzles and hazards, comment, react through their traits and can be
  wounded. Some quests need a particular companion (for example, Runa to judge a mast timber, or
  Senán to read a carved stone).

## 7. Downed, not dead, and scripted deaths (Books 1, 2, 5, 6, 10)

- **Downed friendly characters generally survive** if the steerer brings them back to the boat
  or the village before the scene ends (on a journey, a safe camp or waystation counts). Downed allies do not bleed out on a timer; carrying them
  is slow and leaves the carrier unable to fight. Enemies do not finish off the downed, except
  berserkers and a few named cruel enemies, and those do so with a visible warning.
- **Left behind** at the end of a scene, a downed ally is captured or killed, according to the
  enemy (captives can be rescued or ransomed later).
- Crew permadeath now comes mainly from being left behind, from berserkers and cruel enemies, and
  from **scripted deaths**. The *Rán's mercy* option still prevents unscripted deaths.
- **Scripted deaths and death-choices (PROPOSAL):**
  - **Rán's debt:** Thorkel's bargain (2.2 twist 1) comes due: someone must go into the sea.
    Gudrun offers herself; the steerer can accept, choose otherwise, or refuse and pay another way
    (the ship, the household's luck or a later life).
  - **The Return:** someone must hold the cairn door as the tide rises. Whoever stays dies unless
    the party found the spring-room passage first.
  - **Bran's reckoning:** a monk's family demands Bran's life as blood-price. Bran can go to his
    death, be exiled, or be defended at the cost of the alliance.
  - **Thrand's proof:** Thrand challenges Asgrim to prove Heid's crimes. Without the evidence,
    he dies in the duel.
  - **The defence of Skarvholm:** whoever leads the watch on the night of the attack may die
    holding the landing, depending on preparation.
  - **Berserk deaths:** a berserker like Kári can die charging alone, through a trait.
  - **The washer at the ford** (finale) names who will die; changing orders can save them, at a
    cost elsewhere.
- **Every death is consequential:** grief (happiness falls household-wide), lost skills and jobs,
  changed relationships, a grave to tend, songs at the fire, ghosts or fetches where the dead had
  unfinished business, story branches that close, and enemies who mock or allies who honour.

## 8. Raids, defences and attacks by land and sea (Books 1, 2, 6, 10)

- **Raids** by sea: stealth or all-out assault (2.2 section 7).
- **Attacks on enemy settlements by land** once on the mainland: march overland with the
  household and allies, and assault a camp, farm or fort, with the same formation and objective
  rules.
- **Sea-borne attacks** on coastal settlements: land in force from the ships, including mainland
  coastal places (Carrick, Kilbrecc, Saltvik's outer harbour, Crowwick, Orm's outposts).
- **Defences** of the homestead and the mainland home: watch, warning, preparation (walls, traps,
  stakes, archers on platforms), then the battle.
- Each is a gate when it is a story event, and optional otherwise.

## 10. Subtitles: speech and information (Books 2, 8)

- Subtitles show two kinds of line, and they must look different:
  - **Speech:** what someone says. Speaker name, colon, the words, in upright type:
    `Hild: Here! Over here, past the rocks!`
  - **Information:** sounds, directions and other cues that aren't speech. *Italic*, with no
    speaker prefix and no quotation marks: *Hild, calling from the west*; *Surf breaking on the
    rocks*; *A horn, far off to the north*.
- Information lines are short (under eight words), in the present tense, and give a direction
  when it helps the player find the source.
- A speaker the player hasn't met is named by what they appear to be ("Monk:", "Stranger:",
  "Ardcarn guard:") until introduced. Hidden identities (Nessa's stance, disguised enemies) are
  never given away by a subtitle name.
- When both kinds show at once, the information line sits above the speech line.
- The italic must be a real italic of the caption font, or a hand-made slanted variant if the
  font is a pixel font. It must be crisp at 1x: no blurred, synthetic skew.
- Settings let the player turn information captions off separately from speech subtitles.
- Every caption string is tagged `SPEECH` or `INFO`. Content lint checks that `SPEECH` strings

## 12. The run cycle (Book 7; art lint in Book 2)

This is vital. It applies to every character who runs, in every facing and every build.

- **Frames:** eight per facing (as 2.2): contact, down, passing, suspension, then the same for the
  other leg. Frame timing comes from the run speed in balance.json, so feet never skate.
- **Momentum:** the torso tilts forward 1 to 2 px for the whole run, with head and shoulders ahead
  of the hips.
- **Suspension:** one frame per stride (two per cycle) with **both feet clear of the ground**. The
  shadow stays on the ground and shrinks by 1 px.
- **Knees, never stiff:**
  - On the down frames, the leading knee bends deeply to take the weight, and the whole torso
    drops 1 to 2 px.
  - On the forward swing, the knee drives up high with the leg folded tight.
  - After push-off, the trailing heel kicks up high behind the body.
- **Arms:** elbows bent to about 90 degrees. Fists pump up to chest height on the forward swing,
  and elbows drive far back on the back swing, opposite to the legs.
- **Secondary motion:** hair, the tunic hem, the cloak and sash ends trail a frame behind the
  body, to show drag, and settle over 1 to 2 frames after stopping. Headwind from the wind system
  makes the trail stronger.
- **Facings:** side views show all of this most clearly. Front and back views show it through
  the bob and torso drop, the foreshortened knee lift, the heel kick showing behind the legs, and
  the arms pumping.
- **Variants:** with a weapon drawn or a shield up, the pump is reduced on the occupied arm.
  Carrying a load or a downed ally uses its own heavy gait. The heavy build has deeper down frames
  and a shorter flight.
- **Starting and stopping:** a one-frame lean into the start, and a short skid and settle on
  stopping.
- **Checks:**
  - Art lint confirms each run strip has a suspension frame per stride with both feet at least
    1 px above the ground line, and a torso drop of 1 to 2 px on the down frames.
  - The stage report includes GIFs at 1x and 4x of the walk and run, in every facing, for both
    steerer models and the heavy build, shown side by side.
